# PART 1 — THE PERSON CARD: what must be captured, and how

## 1.1 The two things people cannot self-report

Across US academic study of working stylists and Ukrainian intake practice independently, exactly two
profile fields are systematically wrong when asked directly: **body shape** and **colouring**. Both
are verified by observation instead.

- **Size.** The size a person names and the size they actually buy diverge routinely. Stylists check
  what is in the wardrobe rather than trusting the answer. Ukrainian intake forms use the size
  question deliberately as an honesty probe — an experienced stylist can determine size without the
  form, so the answer's value is in showing how accurately the person sees themselves.
- **Colouring.** Never ask for a self-diagnosed "season". Screen rendering and ambient light make
  self-diagnosis unreliable, and the season taxonomy is not a property of the person in the first
  place (§7.3). Capture instead a **goal near the face** — add warmth, add freshness, add contrast.

*Force:* `strong` for "verify, don't ask" — converging practitioner evidence from two cultures.
*Breaks:* if no photograph is available, the fallback is a stated goal, not a stated diagnosis.

## 1.2 Fields that are inferred, and fields that are asked

Inferred, never asked: income, wealth signals, travel frequency, status. High-end practice reads these
from context to avoid an uncomfortable question.

**Asked, and set by the person: budget.** This is a genuine divergence between US premium practice
(infers budget) and Ukrainian practice (asks; the client names a per-item ceiling). Ukrainian practice
also treats the shopping budget as separate from the service fee. For this system the resolution is to
**ask**, because a user-set budget is a gate the user owns, and an inferred budget is a guess that
silently filters the catalogue.

*Force:* `hard` — budget is a user-owned gate, not a model inference.

## 1.3 The three words

A field with three slots, each functionally assigned rather than freely chosen. Originated by Allison
Bornstein; the structure is stable across her own accounts and independent retellings.

| Slot | Derived from | Function |
|---|---|---|
| **Practical** | the wardrobe "regulars" — what is actually worn every day **out of utility and comfort** | describes current reality |
| **Aspirational** | saved images, Pinterest boards, style muses | direction of travel |
| **Emotional** | how favourite outfits made the person feel | mood the clothing should evoke |

**The reason inside the practical word is load-bearing.** The regulars are worn *out of utility and
comfort* — so the practical word can encode a **constraint** rather than a taste. A person who wears
sneakers daily because she walks everywhere has "casual" as a practical word that describes her
commute, not her preference. Capturing the reason alongside the word is what makes §3.3 possible.

**The aspirational word is a modifier over the practical base, not a second target.** Stated
explicitly in the method: it *coexists with* the favourite basics and resolves the tension between the
concrete and the abstract — the person does **not** need to jettison practical pieces. In system terms
it is a re-ranking signal applied to the base specification, not a competing specification.

**Words are scenario-conditional, not one constant per person.** Documented case: one client's daytime
words were *classic, minimal, elegant with a bit of fashion*; her evening word was *disco ball*. The
field may carry a day/evening dimension.

**Words alone are insufficient.** Practitioner limit, stated directly: words mean very different
things to different people. A word field must be calibrated against stimuli (§2) before it is trusted.

*Force:* `default` for the three-slot structure; `hint` for slot ordering — sources disagree on
whether aspirational or emotional comes second, which suggests order is not functional.

**Known weakness in the supporting evidence.** Celebrity three-word readings (Birkin: *simple, casual,
sexy*; Kardashian: *exaggerated, fitted, sculptural*) are commonly cited as showing that strong
triples are contrastive and stretch the space. But per the method's own construction, aspirational and
emotional words derive from the subject's private boards and feelings, so they cannot be read from
outside. The celebrity readings therefore cannot support claims about the method's structure. Treat
"strong triples are contrastive" as `hint` until tested on real triples.

**Named forks, counted rather than merged.** One practitioner replaces the emotional slot with a
functional/personal word. Another derives words from personality and favourite quotations instead of
from the wardrobe. Three procedures, one output shape.

## 1.4 Constraints

**Hard-nos are an absolute filter, never a low weight.** A categorical "never" — a fabric allergy, a
colour that will not be worn, a silhouette that is refused — removes items from the candidate set. It
does not down-rank them. Conflating the two is the failure that produces a recommendation the person
has already said no to.

*Force:* `hard`. This is not an empirical claim; it is a definition of what "no" means.

**Existing wardrobe as anchor.** Outfits are built from what exists, not from zero. The most common
concrete request stylists receive after occasion-dressing is "combine what I already own", and its
characteristic failure is the belief that pieces must *match precisely* — producing "closet orphans",
items with tags still on that the owner never worked out how to wear.

## 1.5 Occasion and life stage

**Occasion is the single most frequent driver of requests** — roughly a third of logged client
requests in the one academic study of online styling sessions. Its characteristic sub-pattern is
**"dress for X but not look Y"**: business-casual but not boring; winter but still elegant. The job is
to fuse contextual appropriateness with personal style, and the "not Y" half is as specified as the
"for X" half.

Capture the occasion profile as **shares of real life**, not as aspiration: what the person actually
dresses for, weighted.

**Life transition is a real trigger; age is not.** People seek styling at transitions — new job,
return to work, role change, pregnancy, weight change, relocation, a new life stage. Model this as
**context that shifts occasion mix, body, and goal priority**, never as a demographic filter on
styles. "Dress your age" is folklore and was publicly retracted by the practitioner who popularised it.

*Force:* `soft`, and explicitly falsifiable — does knowing the transition improve the recommendation
*beyond* occasion + body + goal? It may be redundant. Untested.

## 1.6 Character and risk tolerance

A real axis, captured in both traditions: attention *not drawn ↔ drawn*; trend ↔ individuality;
importance of comfort. Underlying finding from stylist interviews: many clients dress "like everyone
else" because they fear standing out, and consequently express their taste in their **home interiors
rather than their wardrobe**. Where an interior is visible it is a stronger taste signal than the
closet.

**Trend is secondary for most people.** The common formulation is *not required to be on-trend, but
not visibly outside it.* Trend requests were a small minority of logged sessions.

# PART 2 — ELICITATION

## 2.1 Stimulus beats abstract question

Never ask "what is your style?". Show garments and outfits; collect reactions. Revealed preference
beats stated preference — this is the same finding as §1.1, applied to taste rather than measurement.

**Forced A-vs-B beats an isolated like.** A binary choice between two stimuli discriminates axes and
suppresses acquiescence bias; an isolated "do you like this" does neither.

**Probes must discriminate, not please.** A stimulus set that everyone reacts to identically carries
zero information. Cover the axes to their extremes — loud↔quiet, minimal↔maximal, classic↔trend,
covered↔revealing, casual↔occasion — because tolerance for the edges separates taste better than the
centre does. A probe set skewed toward one market segment poisons the prior.

This is the same disease as modal LLM output: the median discriminates nothing.

**Moodboard reaction is a working probe in top practice** — conversation about films, books, art, then
moodboards, then recording what the person reacts to. Notably, the same practitioner reports having no
go-to designers, because the answer depends on body, style and personality.

*Force:* `default` for stimulus-over-question — converging academic and practitioner evidence.
**`8–12 adaptive probes` is `[folkloric]`** — no source, no measurement. It is a guess about where
information gain saturates.

## 2.2 Show, don't describe

In logged styling conversations the exchange is image-dense: roughly three images per conversation,
and the large majority of exchanges carry an image. Image + text pairs pin down what neither pins
alone.

⚠ These proportions are quoted from a secondary retelling of the CHI paper; the paper itself has not
been read. Treat the direction as sound and the numbers as unverified.

## 2.3 Output of elicitation

Not a label. A **distribution** over sub-types, a set of **goal weights** (which may themselves be
occasion-conditional — timeless for the base, on-trend for going out), a set of **constraints**, and an
explicit record of **where the model is still uncertain**.

Sharp rejections during probing are candidate hard-nos and should be surfaced as such rather than
absorbed as weights.

*Force:* `hard` for distribution-not-label — hard-coding a single tag is a known architectural failure,
and the largest commercial operation in this space represents taste as a distribution over up to five
style types out of ten.

## 2.4 Show the read, allow correction

Show the person the model's inference about them in plain language — "this reads as restrained and
capsule-oriented, is that right?" — and let them overwrite it. This gives agency, immediate
calibration, and trust in one move. The person can always overwrite the model's conclusion about them.

## 2.5 Build order

Onboarding is high-leverage — a wrong initial prior propagates through everything downstream — **and**
easy to over-engineer before anything can consume it. The correct order is: a composer that can
consume a prior, then a minimal onboarding (a few probes, hard-nos, consent), then behavioural
learning, then a rich adaptive onboarding.

*Force:* `hard`, methodological. Do not build an elaborate style quiz while the composer cannot
consume its output.

# PART 3 — GARMENT MECHANICS FROM PRACTITIONERS

Every entry: condition → action → effect. Status of the whole block: practitioner self-report. No
outcome validation exists for any of them. Default force `hint` unless stated.

## 3.1 Proportion operators

**Half-tuck / French tuck.**
*Condition:* top is loose, floaty or oversized (**not** form-fitting); bottom is mid- or high-rise.
*Action:* tuck the front only; leave sides and back loose.
*Effect (as the source states it):* defines the waistline while preserving the drape of a longer,
looser top; leaves the garment loose at the back, balancing the outfit's proportions. Offered for the
case where a top is too loose to tuck fully, or where a full tuck reads too conservative.
*Breaks:* form-fitting tops (explicitly scoped out); low rise (nothing to tuck into).

> **Correction carried forward.** The widely repeated mechanism "makes legs look longer" is **not** in
> the authored source — it appears only in secondary styling blogs reconstructing it from the
> rule-of-thirds argument. The corpus previously recorded it as the practitioner's own. It is removed.
> This matters twice over, because the leg-lengthening effect is separately known to be an inverted-U
> with a low ceiling rather than a monotone gain.

*Force:* `hint`. Waist-definition mechanism `[sourced]`; leg-length claim struck.

**One violation of volume.**
*Condition:* exactly one oversized element in the outfit.
*Action:* close the remaining proportions — an extra-wide-shouldered jacket must not also be long, and
pairs with a narrow bottom; a super-baggy bottom pairs with a fitted top or an explicit waist.
*Force:* `hint` `[folkloric]` — source is the paywalled MasterClass course; not verified.

**Trouser length, top length, taper.** Default trousers as long as possible under the shoe for an
unbroken leg line; crop and capri as a deliberate shortening move; base top terminating at the hip
line; taper to the ankle. All four `[folkloric]` — no readable authored source, no tolerances, no
numbers. **They must not enter slot defaults**: a default is an unmarked hard rule, and "as long as
possible" and "at the hip line" have no measurable threshold.

## 3.2 Footwear register

**Pointed toe.** Pointed shoes create the illusion of longer legs. The practitioner states this
flatly, with no conditions: *"I didn't make the game. I just want you to win."*

> **Correction carried forward.** The corpus recorded a pairing condition — that the effect works only
> together with a hem length that does not break the line. That condition is not in the source. It is
> a conservative addition (it narrows the rule), but it is still unsourced and must not be presented
> as the practitioner's. A practitioner's *always* is a self-report, not a measurement; the underlying
> leg-lengthening ceiling applies regardless of who states it.

*Force:* `hint`.

**Deliberate register mismatch ("wrong shoe").**
*Action:* a shoe drawn from a different formality register than the rest — a sneaker with tailoring, a
heel with denim shorts, a chunky trekking sandal with a suit.
*Effect, in the originator's words:* the shoe, removed from its functional reading, *"signals that
there is some intention and choice, and therefore gives your look some personality."* Her broader
claim: what makes a look feel interesting and personal is an accessory that feels slightly *off* or
mismatched with the rest.
*Antecedents, acknowledged by her:* a long-standing runway device — Chanel, Simone Rocha, GANNI. Her
own framing of what she contributes: *"a way to put language to a visual idea."*

> **Correction carried forward — the gates are invented.** The three conditions the corpus attaches —
> that the mismatch is the *only* source of tension, that the rest of the outfit is quiet and
> coherent, and that there is one such gesture per outfit — appear in **no** primary statement. They
> are inference. One is actively contested in the same discourse: a published essay on the theory
> argues that a haircut, a necklace and an oversized tee stack alongside the shoe, concluding that *a
> lot of wrong comes together to make a right*.

*Force:* `hint` `[folkloric]` for the gates; the effect itself is an attribution claim about a
viewer, and no viewer has ever been asked.

*Effect on code:* this licenses a **remark**, not a block, against the ±1 footwear-formality veto. On
current all-T3 constants that veto already emits a remark rather than a block, so the practical delta
today is zero. Recorded so it is not re-derived.

**Independent convergence worth noting.** A second practitioner, not downstream of the first, frames
the same mechanism as *push/pull* — that everything good stylistically has friction between elements.
This is the only place in theme 6 where two genuinely independent voices state the same mechanism.

## 3.3 Fixed slot as seed

*Condition:* one slot is hard-constrained by daily life — must be a sneaker, because of walking.
*Action:* do not drag the rest of the outfit into that slot's register. Hold the slot fixed and build
the person's favourite pieces from the opposite register around it.
*Documented case:* a client wore sneakers for practical city-walking reasons and disliked that her
outfits therefore read sporty. Her three favourite outfits were re-styled *with* the sneaker; the
sneaker became a through-line and, in the stylist's words, *"now looks like a choice… an intentional
styling decision"*, with the look reading more genuinely as her own.

This is the one documented case behind the register-mismatch idea, and its shape is **constraint
re-read as intent** — not a general licence to mismatch. It connects directly to §1.3: the practical
word derived from *utility and comfort* is exactly how such a constraint is detected.

*Force:* `default` — it is a search strategy, not a claim about appearance, and its failure mode is
merely a wasted branch.

## 3.4 Colour, print, texture

**Print ladder.** Four foundation prints: stripe, dot, animal, floral. First step into mixing: a
striped tee with a dotted shirt layered over; or a black suit with a paisley shirt replacing the white
one. Mixing enters either **as a layer on top** or **by swapping a neutral slot for a print inside an
otherwise quiet frame**.

**Rung below the bottom rung — colour blocking.** For a person whose hard-nos include prints entirely,
colour blocking delivers the visual-interest function without triggering the constraint: *"colour
blocking is a great way to wear a solid without actually wearing a solid — a lot of people say 'I'm
afraid of prints'."* Without this the system has nothing at all to offer a print-averse profile.

**Sparkle placement as gaze direction.** Jewellery carrying shine is placed in the zone to be lit — a
rivière at the throat and décolletage. Jewellery is selected to *blend, not take over* relative to the
whole.

**Register tuning outside the garment slots.** A formal or over-the-top dress is shifted toward
contemporary and casual by simple hair and make-up; added **texture** (lace gloves) adds a dimension
without adding a colour or a second focus. The dial: dress fixed, register rotated by hair × make-up ×
jewellery.

> **Revised — make-up is no longer out of scope.** This paragraph used to end "for this system hair
> and make-up are out of scope, but jewellery and gloves are live slots." That is now false for
> make-up: colours for lips, eyelids and cheeks are accepted as **named profile features** (§3.8
> below). Hair was never out of scope — multiple hair tones have been a profile input throughout.
> The line survived because nothing tested it.

## 3.5 Interest budget: person against garment

*Condition:* the wearer carries the look herself — striking appearance, strong personal contrast, a
statement haircut.
*Action:* the dress goes maximally simple.
*Stated reasoning:* she *"doesn't need distractions."*
*Inversion:* a quieter presence moves the interest into the clothing.

The consequence for architecture: **the focus of an outfit may be the person**, in which case the
garment slots default to support rather than competing for attention.

## 3.6 Wardrobe composition, from two named practitioners

These are purchase and composition heuristics. They become live only when the system ingests an
existing wardrobe.

**Quarters partition.** Split the wardrobe into four: neutral basics, solid colours, prints, textures.
Stated purpose: with that ratio, mixing and matching does not jam.

> **Correction carried forward.** This is **not** a rival to a 70/30 neutral-to-colour ratio. 70/30 is
> one axis, neutral ↔ colour. Quarters is a four-class partition of content in which print and texture
> are separate classes and colour is one of four. They have no common denominator and cannot be
> averaged, ranked, or A/B tested against each other. Use quarters as a **coverage check over content
> classes** — does the candidate set contain all four — which answers a combinatorial question, not an
> appearance question.

**Multiply a verified fit.** When a pair of jeans fits, buy two and hem one to flats and one to heels.
When a shirt fits *"like a glove"* **and the bust is hard to fit**, buy the same model in several
colours and prints. Governing principle stated in the same passage: *stick with what you know* means
stick with the **sizes and shapes** that work — the reusable unit is the fit envelope, not the garment.

The bust half is a **landmark-conditional rule**, not a general one, and should fire on the
bust–underbust delta the profile already carries.

**Connective tissue.** *"Find connectors that will look good with two or more things already in your
closet. Buy the piece that gives you more mileage out of your wardrobe, rather than buying more
pieces."* Threshold **≥ 2 existing pairings** `[folkloric]`. Note that a second, different threshold
exists elsewhere in the corpus — ≥ 3 assembled outfits per item — with a different denominator.
Two named constants, neither measured. Both recorded rather than silently reconciled.

**Fitting-room discipline.** Never buy without trying on. In the fitting room assemble a **whole
outfit**, even when buying one item, because *women buy pieces, not outfits*. Shop on a schedule with
a needs list, not at the last minute under event pressure.

**Durability filters.** Before buying: *can I see myself in this in fifteen years?* Denim without rips,
crystals or print splatter, in classic shades — black, dark indigo, mid blue — lasts. A belt should be
thin and decorative; **if the belt is holding the trousers up, that is a fit failure** and the garment
belongs at a tailor. The belt test is a cheap binary fit diagnostic.

**Alteration risk is asymmetric.** Taken-in-too-small is nearly unrecoverable; the emergency fix is
cutting the lining and inserting a gusset, after which the wearer keeps her arms down all evening.
Therefore alter with the margin toward *slightly looser*.

## 3.7 Evening and event

**Danceability as a hard functional gate.** Wedding-guest constraints: not white; not too revealing;
and mandatorily — you must be able to dance in it. Movement is a functional gate on any evening look,
not a nicety.

**Best angle is a property of the garment.** Every dress has a winning angle — side, hip popped,
straight on — and it is worth knowing before the camera. Direct translation for this system:
**try-on renders should choose the angle that suits the garment**, rather than defaulting to a single
frontal view.

**Light outfit kit.** A white or light look implies a stain pen in the evening bag.

## 3.8 Make-up as an **input**, not an output

This system does not advise make-up. It **reads** it, because a face arrives already made up and
the clothing decision has to be made against the face as it will actually be seen.

**Make-up colours are separate features, one voice each.** Lips, eyelids and cheeks enter the
profile as named features with their own hue arcs, never averaged into one "make-up colour". This
is the same commitment already made for hair: a face with a wine lip and grey-plum shadow has two
colour statements in two different places on the hue circle, and their mean describes neither.
Justification for the single shared palette rather than a parallel make-up module: Theme 2,
R-MUA-02 (Sci\ART / Scaman — one palette across garments, make-up and hair).

**Given versus chosen — the distinction that must survive to the output.** Skin, hair and eyes are
**given**: the person has them. Make-up is **chosen**: it is a decision already taken, and it can
itself be the wrong one. If a lipstick arc is given the same authority as a skin arc, the system
will dress the wearer to agree with today's lipstick as confidently as it dresses her to agree with
her own colouring — and will lock in an error rather than correct it. Every make-up-derived window
therefore travels marked as chosen and can be dropped independently of the person's own features.
The same marking propagates to resonance rows, or a chosen colour would re-enter through the back
door while the direct row was honestly labelled.

**What make-up is allowed to do, and what it is not.**
- *Allowed:* raise the contrast the face can carry near it (K-CLR-03). The lever is now
  **measured** as |ΔL\*| between the make-up zone and the face zone rather than granted flat — a
  nude close to the wearer's own face tone adds almost nothing, a dark lip adds a full step.
  **Ceiling: one step.** The rule grants one step, so measurement may only show that less than a
  step is present; it may never grant more than the rule allows.
- *Allowed:* exclude a large garment that repeats the make-up colour (Theme 2, K-MUA-01).
- *Not allowed:* move the hue axis of the recommended palette. No source supports it — see
  Theme 2, Layer 6, where the absence of any measured make-up↔clothing study is recorded.

**What to ask, and what not to.** Ask for colours as **swatches or photographs**, the same way skin
and hair are asked (Theme 6 §2.1 — stimulus beats abstract question). Do **not** ask "what is your
undertone", and do **not** carry the wrist-vein test that accompanies this advice throughout the
popular layer: it has no reliability data behind it in any source located.

**Where this breaks.**
1. **A selfie with make-up is a biased measurement of the person underneath.** Cosmetics move
   facial contrast substantially and in the same direction as the sex difference (Theme 2, R-PC-03),
   so a made-up photo reports a shifted person. The system currently has **no way to tell** whether
   the face it measured was made up. This is a live hole and it affects every rule keyed to contrast.
2. **The lever is not area-weighted.** Lips are a few percent of the face; the |ΔL\*| lever ignores
   that. The one-step ceiling caps the overstatement but does not remove it. Area weighting on the
   face is genuinely open — R-CHEV-18 shows lips influence a field far larger than they occupy, so
   naive chroma × area is the wrong correction.
3. **The person's own loudness budget is not yet coupled.** If make-up already spends the loud slot,
   the garment budget should shrink (Theme 2, R-MUA-04, and §3.5 above). The input exists; the
   coupling does not.
4. **No verdict.** No woman has yet said "I would wear this" about anything the make-up path
   produced.

# PART 4 — EXPLANATION: how the system speaks

## 4.1 The grammar

Extracted structure, common to every practitioner examined:

> **[effect] + because [mechanism] + provided [condition]**

Reference instance: the half-tuck balances proportions / because it opens the waist point under a
loose top / provided the top is loose and the rise is mid-to-high.

Other legitimate forms in the same grammar: **subtractive** — "she is best in maximally simple dresses;
she doesn't need distractions"; **parametric refusal** — declining a universal answer because it
depends on where, when, and whether there is a theme.

## 4.2 What explanation is for

The practitioner's own account of why she names her methods: *"a way to put language to a visual
idea"* — clients often see a look or a vibe they like but do not know **why it works or how to
reproduce it.**

So the function of an explanation in practice is: **name the mechanism so that the decision becomes
reproducible.** That is precisely the function of a verbaliser plus rule-trace, arrived at
independently.

## 4.3 Templates

- mechanism form: *"works because … provided …"*
- intent framing: *"reads as a deliberate choice"*
- **"conscious camouflage"** instead of "hide" — a practitioner's own formulation, evolved through
  living with psoriasis, arthritis and age: *not hiding, but conscious camouflage.* Camouflage is
  legitimate when it is **her** deliberate choice, never a system default.
- opening question: *"how do you want to feel today?"*

## 4.4 Explain the refusal too

In practice a stylist gives an extended comment on **every** item, including the ones rejected. The
system must expose the "why" on the proposal **and** on the rejection. Trace already makes it visible
where a decision broke; it must be surfaced.

## 4.5 Three modes of delivery

A client's description of a stylist she trusts: *knows when to push, when to protect, and when to say
"trust me."* Three distinct registers for the same recommendation.

## 4.6 Saying no is a craft

Explain why; always offer a better alternative; use humour, hedging, personalisation. A working
technique: ask the person to put on their favourite outfit first, setting a high bar. And: **veto even
an objectively good garment if the body language says she will not wear it** — authenticity beats
flattery.

## 4.7 The three words as a post-assembly predicate

The originator's operational form: *"think of your three words as a checklist… call upon your words
when you are feeling stuck; look in the mirror and ask, are each of my three words represented in this
look?"*

This is per-outfit, post-assembly, and boolean over three tags. It has the exact shape of the
verification layer: three labels, each must map to at least one item in the assembled outfit, and the
word that fails **names the repair**. It is the cheapest verification predicate in the whole theme and
requires no new data beyond the profile field itself.

*Force:* `default` as a check; the mapping from word to garment tag is unbuilt.

# PART 5 — FORCE CALIBRATION: two axes, not one

The corpus invariant is **force ≤ verification** — how strongly a rule is stated is bounded by its
evidence.

A second, orthogonal axis is documented by a practitioner reflecting on twenty years of rule-giving.
Asked whether she regretted the rigid televised rules, she explained: it was important to establish
authority, and — the operative part — *"I wanted those rules to feel very strict because the people we
worked with would not be good at improvising if the rules weren't very clear cut. So it was very
important that we gave very strict and steadfast rules, making the trail of breadcrumbs easier to
follow."*

**Rule rigidity was calibrated to the user's improvisation ability, not to the rule's truth.** The same
rule was `hard` for a novice and would be `soft` for someone who improvises well.

These are different quantities and the corpus currently collapses them into one number. A rule can be
well-evidenced and still need to be delivered as a guard rail to a confident user; or weakly evidenced
and still need to be delivered firmly to someone who has asked for a decision rather than options.

**Where the same practitioner landed after the revision:** *"Now my advice is to understand what a
person wants to convey and feel, and help them get there. I'll happily put up guard rails so you
don't drive off the road, but I won't choose the make, model and colour of the car for you."*

That is the architecture: interpreted gates plus user autonomy, against prescriptive generation.

**Anti-lesson from the same trajectory:** the rigid demographic rules — gender rules, "dress your age",
"cover that" — aged publicly and required the author's own retraction. Parameters and lookup packages,
never constants.

*Falsifiable test for the second axis:* take one rule already at `default`, render it twice — once as a
directive, once as a guard rail with the reasoning exposed — and check whether the wear verdict
differs. If it does not, this axis is not worth building and this section should be deleted.

# PART 6 — AUTONOMY AND WELLBEING RAILS

Compiled from converging practitioner norms in both traditions. These are hard rails, not preferences.

- **Never criticise.** Advise. The person's right to use the advice or not is explicit.
- **Emphasis, not flaws.** Body questions ask what to emphasise, never what to hide. Colour asks for a
  goal, never a verdict. Budget without judgement.
- **Never push.** Pressing is explicitly named an anti-norm. Disagreement is negotiated, treated as a
  **signal**, never overcome by persuasion.
- **Budget is a user-set gate.**
- **Any soft default is user-overridable.**
- **Sensitive fields are optional**, delicately worded, with the reason for asking stated. No
  flaw-focus. Never feed negative self-talk.
- **Consent gates** on sensitive fields and on any local-first preference — consent turns a nudge into
  something chosen rather than imposed, which removes the trust trap.

**Divergence is prevented, not resolved.** Front-loaded specificity plus **agreement checkpoints
between stages** — key anchors confirmed *before* full assembly, not at the end. One-shot delivery
without agreement is against practice.

**This is a deliberate departure from the trained industry default.** Ukrainian styling education
teaches "figure correction" — hide wide hips, hide the stomach, reduce or add volume — as the core of
the curriculum. The emphasis framing is a conscious departure and a potential differentiator: *we are
not looking for flaws in you.* Hold the value; translate it into accepted vocabulary rather than
importing the flaw frame.

Softening precedent from the strongest practitioners in that same tradition: one reports having typed
roughly a thousand clients and never having seen an unattractive one; another defines the task as
emphasising strengths and teaching the person to do it herself.

# PART 7 — QUARANTINE: what the sources forbid building

## 7.1 Typology, cancelled by its own author

The founder of the most-copied body typology published a 2025 successor to his 1987 book stating
**"NO BODY PARTS"**, dropping feature-matching in favour of self-discovery through personal-line
sketching (how fabric drapes on the body), and explicitly reframing the 1987 physical descriptions as
historical reference rather than a self-typing checklist. Type count moved from 13 toward 10.

The operation a compiler needs — tagging bodies and garments with a type — is the operation the author
now forbids. The system is uncompilable by its own design.

⚠ Sourced from a secondary summary of the book, not the book. Load-bearing enough that it should be
checked before being cited as settled.

## 7.2 Monogenealogy: agreement between schools is one voice, not many

**Colour line:** Caygill (1940s–50s; a holistic system with dozens of named sub-seasons, individual
palettes) → Jackson, *Color Me Beautiful* (1980), which simplified it to four seasons with binary
draping and became a franchise → every modern twelve-season system.

**Founder's own admission — ⚠ ATTRIBUTION DID NOT SURVIVE VERIFICATION (run 3, 2026-09-06).**
The quotation *"nothing more than a convenient artifice"* was attributed to Jackson,
*Color Me Beautiful* (1980). Three verification passes did **not** confirm it in the book; only
the four-season system itself is confirmed. The quotation is therefore withdrawn — the same
wording already applied to the Chanel attribution in K-ACC-13. **Closed for research.**
What remains standing without it: the four-season system is a taxonomy of convention, not a
mechanism (R-CHEV-12), and that claim never depended on the quotation.

**Body/archetype line:** Belle Northrup, *An Approach to the Problem of Costume and Personality* (1936,
Columbia Teachers College — yin/yang as a universal aesthetic scale) → Morton → McJimsey, *Art in
Clothing Selection* (six archetypes: dramatic, natural, gamine, classic, ingénue, romantic) →
Kitchener (seven essences) → Kibbe (1987, thirteen types in five families, ingénue removed).

Both lines were born in academic home economics and fossilised in commercial systems after the
academy moved on. This is not folklore that infiltrated education; it is 1930s–60s education that
calcified into franchises.

**The methodological consequence is a rule about rules.** The triangulation heuristic — agreement of
two or three schools raises confidence — silently assumes independence. For typologies that assumption
is false by construction: every school descends from one or two mid-century ancestors, so their
agreement is a single voice repeated in chorus. Genealogy must be tracked before agreement is counted.

*Force:* `strong`, meta-methodological.

## 7.3 Documented unreliability

- The same client photograph sent to two virtual analysts produced two entirely different verdicts;
  the practitioner who published the case treats it as part of the process.
- Practitioners publish a whole genre of "when the analysts disagree" pieces, and among the causes they
  name is that **different systems group people differently, emphasising different aspects of colour**.
  So a "season" is a property of the school chosen, not of the person. This is a stronger claim than
  "analysts make mistakes."
- USPTO patent 6,000,407 concedes in its own description that four-season analysis *requires the
  exercise of a great deal of subjective judgment* and *frequently suffers as a result of faulty
  judgments made by inexperienced consultants.*
- The canonical requirement of "in person, in natural light only" makes any disagreement attributable
  to conditions and never to the system — an unfalsifiability shield.
- Professional certification contains **no practical examination in which two consultants must reach
  the same verdict about the same client.** Inter-rater agreement is measured by no mechanism at any
  level of the profession. Poor reliability is structural, not a delivery failure.

## 7.4 What the professional standard actually certifies

The institutional body's core competencies name **design principles** — balance, proportion, scale,
rhythm, emphasis, unity — and **design elements** — line, shape, colour, texture, pattern. The exam is
100 multiple-choice questions; half of it is the artistic domain. The portfolio requires paid-client
invoices and evaluations, business identity, and a code-of-ethics signature.

**Absent from the standard entirely:** seasons, colour types, body-shape letters, the named typologies,
yin/yang, archetypes. The glossary explicitly excludes garment-styling terms. Typologies live in the
continuing-education vendor layer *beneath* the umbrella, not in certified knowledge.

The practical consequence: the elements-and-principles language is structurally isomorphic to an
axis-based system. The rulebook is not against the profession — it sits with the profession's
institutional core against its mass-market layer.

## 7.5 Body language — nothing compilable

- **Power posing:** hormonal effects abandoned by the first author in 2016; behavioural effects null in
  pre-registered replications; only a weak self-report "I feel more confident" survives.
- **7-38-55:** its author restricted the formula in writing to incongruent communication of feelings
  and attitudes. "93% of communication is non-verbal" is an urban legend built on his data.
- **Deception detection from body cues and micro-expressions:** ≈54%, barely above chance.

Zero compilable content. The only real component — how a person is *read* by others — belongs to the
perception literature in the signalling block, not here.

# PART 8 — MARKET LANGUAGE BRIDGE

Roughly eight independent Ukrainian styling schools teach a near-identical curriculum built on exactly
the systems quarantined above: colour typing, contrast, figure types and typing methods (Kibbe and one
other named system), figure correction and optical illusions in dress, style vectors and archetypes,
capsule wardrobe, style-map construction, and clothing psychology.

**This is not independent validation.** It is the same convention arriving from the education industry.
It settles two things:

1. **It is the expected market vocabulary.** A Ukrainian user who has met a stylist or styling content
   knows *кольоротип / тип фігури / посадка / капсула / корекція*. The product must **speak** that
   language while holding the numerical precision of those systems as folklore internally. Principle:
   speak the vernacular, deliver the real core. A label is a lexical bridge; the axes are underneath.
2. **The flaw frame is the trained default**, which makes §6 a conscious departure.

The professional association in that market provides community, a directory and media access — no
standard, no examination, no body of knowledge. Institutionalisation is thinner than the international
body's, which is itself thin.

# PART 9 — FEEDBACK: refusal is the primary signal

At industrial scale the most valuable data point in the cycle is the event "she doesn't like it."

- **Override as data.** A client request that contradicts her own recorded dislike — she "hates
  dresses" but asks for a dress for a wedding — causes the stylist to consciously override algorithmic
  suppression. The override itself is a **labelled example**, not an error to be suppressed.
- **Free text into structure.** Requests and feedback are run through item-feature extraction; the
  training data for that extraction is produced by the stylists themselves reading notes and writing
  out features. Humans teach the model; the model's ranking guides the humans.
- **Confirmation by inaction.** "Here are your sizes on record — no action means confirmed, and there
  are many channels to change it." Keep/return works without words at all.
- **Reactions are written against specification axes, not against SKUs.**

Bounded revision as contract: not "perfect first time" but a **limited rework cycle priced in** — up to
two free revisions plus a fit follow-up is the formalised international version; the local equivalent
is a post-delivery support window of one week to six weeks.

# PART 10 — EMPIRICAL ANCHORS AND CORRECT CITATIONS

Only three items in this theme rest on outcome-measured evidence rather than practitioner self-report.

**Body-shape principles, tested.** The five principles distilled from mainstream styling books —
balance wider shoulders by adding at the hips; balance wider hips by adding at the shoulders; define
the waist; lead the eye to desired features; elongate the silhouette — were tested with outfits built
to the rules versus not, rendered in neutral grey on 3D avatars, judged by roughly 250 raters.
Predictive power **exists but is conditional**: hierarchical (some principles weigh more), shape-
dependent, and magnitude-dependent. The texture of the finding is *real core, folkloric precision*.

**No source exists, by construction:** the eleven onboarding rules. That note declares itself a design

learning, uncertainty sampling, forced choice versus rating, acquiescence bias, revealed versus stated

**Numbers with no source:** *8–12 adaptive probes*; *≥2 connective-tissue pairings*; the quarters
partition; every formality aggregation weight; every wrong-shoe gate; **ΔE00 ≤ 5.0 as "the same
colour"** in K-MUA-01 (T4 folklore — practitioners say "the same colour", not a number).

**Make-up block (§3.8) — what it is and is not.** The practitioner sources behind it are named and
they converge, but **none of them is a worked before/after on a specific woman**: they are stated
rules with stated mechanisms, gathered from published guidance and course material, not from watching
a stylist choose. That is one rung above anonymous copy and one rung below what rule 5 actually asks
for. **Not verified at all:** whether any of it improves an outfit. **Not built:** the detector that
would tell the system a photo was taken with make-up on — without it every contrast-keyed rule is
reading a shifted person and does not know it.

**Nothing in this file has been validated by outcome.** Read is not validated. No rule here has been
applied to a real garment, and no outfit built with it has been worn or judged.

**Error pattern observed across every rule checked against a readable source.** The errors run in both
directions and are asymmetric. **Losses** are specifics — a landmark, a reason, an axis — whose
recovery makes a rule narrower and more usable. **Additions** are gates that make a rule look
better-verified than it is; removing them lowers force. Recovering losses raises usefulness; removing
additions lowers force. Neither produces a new rule.

The three genuinely new items in this file — force as audience latitude, connective tissue, colour
blocking as the print-averse rung — all came from passages **beside** the quoted ones in the same
interviews, attached to no rule. That is where remaining yield lives, if any does: not in re-verifying
what was extracted, but in the paragraphs next to it.

# PART 12 — WHAT THIS THEME NEEDS FROM THE SYSTEM

Theme 6 is about elicitation and dialogue. There is currently no interaction to hold it: the pipeline
entry point takes colour features, occasion parameters and a body argument. It does not take hard-nos,
goal weights, or an occasion profile.

So the most obviously correct rule in this file — **hard-nos are an absolute filter** — has nowhere to
live. The schema gap, not the knowledge gap, is what blocks theme 6.

**Ordered by cost, the three things that would make this file operational:**

1. **Three fields in the profile:** `hard_no` (list, absolute filter), `occasion_profile` (weighted
   shares), `goal_weights` (occasion-conditional). Cheapest, unblocks the most.
2. **Three-word field plus the post-assembly predicate** (§4.7) — reuses the existing verification
   layer; needs a word→tag mapping.
3. **Derived formality** from element polarities rather than a per-SKU tag that feed files do not
   carry. Weights remain a convention until calibrated on 30–50 real catalogue items with a single
   human verdict, reporting the **disagreement** rate.

**Falsifiable prediction, recorded in advance.** Run one real profile against one real catalogue feed,
hand the package to a model, take three outfits and one human verdict of *would wear / would not*.
Prediction: the package fails on **missing fields** — hard-nos and occasion profile — not on rule
quality, and the verdict turns on fit and occasion appropriateness rather than on anything in this
theme. If that holds, the next work is a schema change. If the verdict instead turns on colour or
proportion, this prediction is wrong and the rule layer matters more than argued here.
