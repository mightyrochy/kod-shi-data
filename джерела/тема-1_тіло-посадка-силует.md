# Body Shape & Proportion — Primary-Source Extraction (EN)

**Status:** every number below was read out of the source document itself, not from a summary.
Each source is named with the exact page/table it came from, so any claim can be re-checked in one step.

**What this file is for:** it supplies the measurement layer for `posture/fit` routing — specifically,
whether to add a *high hip* measurement to onboarding, and which classifier to run once it is there.
It does **not** supply styling moves. Styling moves that came with dead citations were dropped (see §7).

**Convention:** all FFIT thresholds are in **inches**, exactly as published. Do not convert them to
centimetres and round — the 3.6″ and 1.193 constants are unrounded fitted values, and rounding
them to cm shifts category boundaries.

## 1. FFIT — what it actually is, and what it is not

Simmons, Istook & Devarajan (2004) built a shape-sorting program at NC State that assigns a
3D body scan to one of **nine** categories from circumference ratios: Hourglass, Top Hourglass,
Bottom Hourglass, Spoon, Rectangle, Diamond, Oval, Triangle, Inverted Triangle.

Verbatim shape definitions, as restated by Sokolowski & Bettencourt (2020, §2.3), who worked
directly from the FFIT papers:

| Shape | Definition |
|---|---|
| Hourglass | proportional bust and hip, with a defined waistline |
| Bottom Hourglass | larger hip than bust, with a defined waistline |
| Top Hourglass | larger bust than hip, with a defined waistline |
| Spoon | significant hip−bust difference, bust-to-waist ratio smaller than Hourglass, and a significant high-hip-to-waist ratio — a "shelf" where the waist drops off sharply, with hip and high hip measuring similarly |
| Triangle | larger hip than bust, no defined waist |
| Inverted Triangle | larger bust than hip, no defined waist |
| Rectangle | similar bust and hip, no defined waistline |
| Diamond | very large midsection; average of stomach, waist and abdomen **larger** than bust |
| Oval | large midsection; average of stomach, waist and abdomen **smaller** than bust |

**Load-bearing caveat, stated by Sokolowski & Bettencourt:** Simmons et al. never documented
*where on the body* the stomach and abdomen measurements are taken. Diamond and Oval are therefore
the two categories that cannot be reproduced from the original paper alone. This is why the 2007
mathematical version omitted them — not because they don't exist.

### 1.1 Sample sizes — the numbers are not interchangeable

Three different N's circulate for "FFIT" and they belong to three different studies:

- **222** — the scans in Part I on which the categories were described. Bottom Hourglass was the
  largest group at 40%, Hourglass 21.6%, Spoon third at 17.1%.
- **253** — the convenience sample, mostly from North Carolina, on which the *software was built*.
- **~6,000+** — the later SizeUSA analysis, which is where the widely quoted "46% rectangle /
  20% pear / 14% apple / 8% hourglass" spread comes from. This is **not** the Part I distribution.

Devarajan & Istook (2004) then validated the software against a **larger** population than the 253
it was trained on, and reported accuracy of **around 90%**, better than a discriminant function
fitted on the training sample.

> Note the direction: the software was *built* on 253 and *validated* on a larger set. Any summary
> that says "validated against 253 women" has it backwards, and inherits a false impression of how
> narrow the validation was.

**Operational use of the 90% figure:** it is the ceiling on classifier confidence, and it applies to
*3D scan measurements*, not to self-measurement with a tape and not to a phone photo. Confidence from
a photo-derived profile must sit below it, not at it.

## 2. The executable formulas (Lee, Istook, Nam & Park 2007)

Lee et al. compared US (SizeUSA) and Korean (SizeKorea) women and produced the mathematical
representation of the FFIT categories that everyone since has actually run. Seven categories;
Diamond and Oval dropped for the reason in §1.

Reproduced from **Sokolowski & Bettencourt (2020), Table 7** — inches, evaluation in this order:

```
Hourglass          (bust−hip) ≤ 1  and (hip−bust) < 3.6  and ((bust−waist) ≥ 9 or (hip−waist) ≥ 10)
Bottom Hourglass   (hip−bust) ≥ 3.6 and (hip−bust) < 10  and (hip−waist) ≥ 9 and (high hip/waist) < 1.193
Top Hourglass      (bust−hip) > 1  and (bust−hip) < 10   and (bust−waist) ≥ 9
Spoon              (hip−bust) > 2  and (hip−waist) ≥ 7   and (high hip/waist) ≥ 1.193
Triangle           (hip−bust) ≥ 3.6 and (hip−waist) < 9
Inverted Triangle  (bust−hip) ≥ 3.6 and (bust−waist) < 9
Rectangle          (hip−bust) < 3.6 and (bust−hip) < 3.6 and (bust−waist) < 9 and (hip−waist) < 10
```

**Order matters.** These are not mutually exclusive predicates; the published form is a cascade
("If … then if …"). Evaluating them in a different order silently changes the answer for
boundary bodies.

### 2.1 Measurement definitions the formulas assume

- **Bust** — around the fullest part, over a properly fitted bra.
- **Waist** — smallest circumference at the natural waist.
- **High hip** — the upper swell of the hip over the pelvic region, roughly **7 inches / 18 cm below
  the natural waist**. This is *not* the widest point.
- **Hip** — largest circumference over the fullest part of the buttocks.

The high-hip definition is the fragile one. It is the only landmark defined by a distance rather
than by a feature, and it is the input on which the Spoon boundary rests entirely.

### 2.2 Prevalence — the label distribution is population-specific

Lee et al. (2007), from the paper's own comparison:

| | Hourglass | Rectangle |
|---|---|---|
| US women (n = 6,310) | 11.8% | 49% |
| Korean women (n = 1,799) | 0.5% | 70.6% |

A 24× difference in Hourglass prevalence between two national samples using the *same formulas*.

Cross-cultural spread from later FFIT applications (compiled in Chrimes et al. 2023, Table II):

- Chinese women 18–35 (n=400): 57% Spoon, 26% Bottom Hourglass
- US women 18–35 (n=340): 44% Bottom Hourglass, 22% Spoon
- African-American college women (n=72): 73.6% pear/triangle, 4.2% Hourglass
- UK women 18–34 (n=30): 43.3% Bottom Hourglass, 33.3% Rectangle, 13.3% Spoon, 6.7% Hourglass

**Where this breaks for us:** there is no Ukrainian FFIT distribution in any of this literature.
Any prior over shape labels for Ukrainian users is currently unfounded. Do not seed one from the
US numbers — the US↔Korea gap shows how far a borrowed prior can be off.

## 3. Plus-size correction (Sokolowski & Bettencourt 2020)

The 2007 formulas carry an undeclared assumption: **the waist is smaller than both bust and hip.**
That holds for most bodies under US size 14 and fails above it.

Observed failure mode, from the paper: many plus-size scans were sorted as Rectangle or Inverted
Triangle by formula, and visual inspection showed those labels were wrong — the waist circumference
exceeded bust or hip. Others matched no category at all.

**Modified formulas, Table 9** — differences from Table 7 in **bold**:

```
Hourglass          unchanged
Bottom Hourglass   unchanged
Top Hourglass      unchanged
Spoon              unchanged
Triangle           (hip−bust) ≥ 3.6 and 0 ≤ (hip−waist) < 9
                   OR (bust−waist) < 0 and (hip−waist) ≥ 0
Inverted Triangle  (bust−hip) ≥ 3.6 and (bust−waist) < 9 and (hip−waist) ≥ 0
Rectangle          (hip−bust) < 3.6 and (bust−hip) < 3.6
                   and 0 ≤ (bust−waist) < 9 and 0 ≤ (hip−waist) < 10
Diamond            (hip−waist) < 0 and (bust−waist) < 0
Oval               (hip−waist) < 0 and (bust−waist) ≥ 0
```

The mechanism is one idea applied four times: **add a non-negativity check**, then let the two
negative cases fall through to Diamond and Oval. The Diamond/Oval split is now defined on
`bust − waist` alone, which sidesteps the undocumented stomach/abdomen landmark from §1.

**Verified reproduction.** I transcribed both formula sets and ran the paper's three worked
subjects (Tables 8 and 10). All three reproduce exactly:

| Subject | 2007 formulas | 2020 formulas | Paper says |
|---|---|---|---|
| #1 | Inverted Triangle | Oval | Inverted Triangle → Oval ✓ |
| #2 | Rectangle | Diamond | Rectangle → Diamond ✓ |
| #3 | Rectangle | Triangle | Rectangle → Triangle ✓ |

**How much this changes**, measured on simulated populations at UA-typical dimensions (N=20,000 each):

| | unclassified, 2007 | unclassified, 2020 | label changed |
|---|---|---|---|
| bust≈94 waist≈74 hip≈100 cm | 0.9% | 0.6% | **3.3%** |
| bust≈112 waist≈100 hip≈118 cm | 1.7% | 0.7% | **12.0%** |

Diamond and Oval are unreachable without the 2020 revision. If the product serves plus sizes at all,
use the 2020 set — 12% of those users get a different label, and the 2007 label is the one the
authors demonstrated to be visually wrong.

### 3.1 Two findings that break "shape is a stable property"

Both from the same paper and both are consequential:

1. **Shape does not persist across a size range.** Sokolowski & Bettencourt state directly that the
   same body shape does not consistently exist throughout a size range. A shape label is therefore
   not an invariant of a person; it is a reading at a body state.
2. **65 size-18 scans were matched against six major US retailers' plus size charts, and 98.46% fitted
   none of them.** Retailer charts assume an Hourglass distribution. Among the plus-size scans studied,
   *none* were Hourglass or Top Hourglass and only 8.7% were Bottom Hourglass.

The second is the strongest single argument in this literature for not reasoning from nominal size.

## 4. Reliability — the measurement definition is part of the classifier

Parker, Hayes, Brownbridge & Gill (2021), *Ergonomics* 64(8), 1035–1051.

Method: **1,679 3D body scans**, each classified twice — once with measurements placed per FFIT's
own guidance, once per **SizeUK's** guidance. Same bodies, same formulas, two landmark conventions.

Findings, in the authors' terms:

- Different interpretations of FFIT's measurement placement put the same body in different shape
  categories in **up to 40% of cases**.
- FFIT is treated as a "gold standard" in ergonomics research without a scientific basis for that status.
- FFIT **omits shoulder measurements**, which have little relationship to body shape as FFIT defines
  it but are vital in garment construction.
- Using FFIT across datasets with differing definitions produces inconsistent conclusions about
  shape differences.

**What this licenses and what it doesn't.** It does *not* say FFIT is wrong. It says the tuple
(formula, landmark convention) is the classifier, and reporting only the formula under-specifies it.

**Operational consequences:**
- The onboarding measurement instructions are not UI copy — they are a classifier parameter. Version
  them alongside the formulas and store which version produced a stored label.
- A shape label imported from another system (a quiz, a retailer, the user's own recollection) is not
  commensurable with one computed here, and must not be merged into the same field.
- 40% disagreement is the reason a shape label must never be a hard gate. At that reliability it
  belongs at hint strength — routing and explanation, not filtering.
- Shoulders are absent from FFIT by construction. If the system needs shoulder geometry — and for
  jacket and sleeve decisions it does — that comes from somewhere else, not from a richer FFIT label.

## 5. What the high-hip measurement actually buys

This is the decision this file exists to settle: onboarding currently collects shoulders, bust,
waist, hips. FFIT needs a fifth measurement. Is it worth asking for?

**Measured.** Holding bust/waist/hip fixed and sweeping high hip across its real anatomical range
(0.86–0.99 of hip circumference), N=40,000 simulated bodies:

```
bodies whose FFIT label depends on the high-hip value:   15.0%

which labels high hip decides between:
  Bottom Hourglass / Spoon    6.68%
  Spoon / Triangle            4.12%
  Rectangle / Spoon           2.51%
  unclassified / Spoon        1.68%
```

**Every single one is a Spoon decision.** High hip enters the formulas exactly twice, and both
times it is the Spoon/not-Spoon boundary. The measurement buys one distinction and nothing else.

**So: is that one distinction worth an extra onboarding question?** The evidence says yes, and it is
concrete rather than taxonomic. Chrimes et al. (2023) put 30 UK women through a body scan and then a
physical bodycon-dress try-on, and recorded fit complaints by shape. Spoon and Bottom Hourglass
women reported the same hip and seat tightness — but Spoon women additionally reported **thigh**
tightness, which Bottom Hourglass women did not. Simmons et al. had already noted wider thighs in
the Spoon shape.

That is a real difference in which garments fail, on a boundary that is invisible without high hip.

### 5.1 Defect in the current implementation

`areas.py` does not measure high hip. It derives it:

```python
"високе_стегно": w["стегна"] * 0.92
```

Consequence, algebraically: `high_hip/waist = 0.92 × hip/waist`, so the FFIT test
`high_hip/waist ≥ 1.193` collapses into `hip/waist ≥ 1.2967`. The independent axis disappears; Spoon
becomes a function of the hip/waist ratio the classifier already has.

Two measured effects:

- **4.2%** of bodies get a different label than they would with a measured high hip.
- **Bottom Hourglass becomes structurally impossible below a waist of 77 cm.** It requires
  `hip − waist ≥ 9″` *and* `hip/waist < 1.2967` simultaneously; with waist ≤ 30.3″ ≈ 77 cm those two
  cannot both hold. Every such woman is forced to Spoon. Bottom Hourglass prevalence halves in
  simulation (1.8% → 0.9%) — and Bottom Hourglass is the *largest* category in the UK and US
  young-adult samples in §2.2.

**Falsifiable next step:** add high hip to onboarding as a measured value (7″ / 18 cm below the
natural waist, over the pelvic bone), with the 0.92 derivation retained only as a labelled fallback
that suppresses the Spoon/Bottom-Hourglass distinction rather than faking it. Then check on real
users whether Spoon-classified women report thigh-fit complaints at a higher rate than
Bottom-Hourglass-classified women. If they don't, the fifth measurement is not earning its question
and should be dropped.

## 6. Fit-failure observations by shape (Chrimes, Boardman, McCormick & Vignali 2023)

*Journal of Fashion Marketing and Management*, DOI 10.1108/JFMM-03-2022-0049.

Design: 30 UK women, 18–34, Size Stream 3D scan → FFIT classification per Lee et al. → try-on of a
black bodycon dress in their usual size → semi-structured interview. Scan **before** try-on, because
the pilot showed try-on-first raised body dissatisfaction and suppressed candid responses.

Observed complaints, by shape:

| Shape | Where the dress failed |
|---|---|
| Rectangle | tightness at the stomach; bust, waist and hips reported as fitting well |
| Bottom Hourglass | tightness at hips and seat |
| Spoon | hips and seat **plus thighs** |
| Triangle | stomach |
| Hourglass | **looseness** at the stomach — the only shape reporting excess rather than tightness |

**Value and limits.** This is one garment type (a stretch bodycon), one age band, n=30, from a
single-country convenience sample. Cell sizes are tiny — Triangle is n=1, Hourglass n=2. Treat these
as *hypotheses about where to look first*, not as rates.

**What it changes anyway:** it gives each shape a named failure zone to check, rather than a generic
"flattering" instruction. The Hourglass result is the useful surprise — the shape retailers fit to
was the one reporting the garment was *too loose* at the stomach, which is a construction fact about
whom the block was drafted for, not a fact about hourglass bodies.

## 7. What was cut from the audited file, and why

The ChatGPT-produced file (6,895 lines) was compared against these primary sources. What did not
survive:

**7.1 Dead citations.** Fifty source attributions are unresolved ChatGPT UI tokens
(`cite␀turn2search16␀`, wrapped in private-use Unicode U+E200/E201/E202). They are not references —
there is no way to recover which page `turn1search55` was. They carry the entire "second audit" and
"third deep-research pass" sections (roughly §120–§170, about half the document). Content there may
be true; none of it is checkable, so none of it can carry more than hint strength, and citing it
would be citing nothing.

**7.2 A "2025 pattern-optimization study" cited without attribution.** *(Amended: the study is named further down this file and is now identified in §14 — Fashion and Textiles 12 (2025), DOI 10.1186/s40691-025-00437-8. The objection below stands and is strengthened: the source is a Korean **menswear** MTM block.)*
Originally recorded as unnamed, supplying a four-factor fit model
(71.4% of variance), height bands P/R/T, drop bands Y/A/B/BB and posture angle thresholds
21.1° / 10.0°. No author, no title, no journal, no DOI. The drop bands are additionally from
**Korean menswear fit models at size 100** — a men's industrial block, imported without comment into
a women's styling corpus. The decomposition idea (proportion / drop / lateral posture / shoulder fit
as separate variables) is sound and worth keeping; the numbers are not usable.

**7.3 The stripe section is a regression on what the project already holds.** The audited file
reports the Thompson & Mikellidou 2011 result (+7.1% height, +4.5% width) and stops. The project's
existing `study-note_01` already carries the two conditions that decide whether the effect applies
at all: Ashida et al. 2013 (the slimming direction holds for already-slim figures and weakens on
fuller bodies) and Koutsoumpis et al. 2021 (garment **lightness** produces an independent effect that
matches or exceeds stripe orientation, modulated by background contrast). Importing the audited
version would delete both gates and leave a rule that fires where it shouldn't.

**7.4 Truism lists.** The section headed "ADDITIONAL VERIFIED OPERATIONAL KNOWLEDGE" is ~400 lines of
telegraphic bullets — "sleeve fit must distinguish circumference from length", "bust size and bust
projection must be separated". No condition, no strength, no failure mode, and no decision that
comes out differently with them than without them. The heading claims verification the content
does not have.

**7.5 What the audited file did have right.** Its FFIT/Concept Wardrobe/Parker section headers are
substantially accurate: the 1,679 scans, the 40% figure, the missing shoulder measurement, and The
Concept Wardrobe's 5%/25% thresholds all check out against source. Its architectural stance —
zones over labels, shape as routing rather than identity — matches what the primary sources support.
What it never did was extract the formulas. It cited FFIT across nine bibliography entries and four
sections without ever reproducing a single executable threshold, which is the only part of FFIT that
does work.

## 8. Not done

Stated as plainly as the findings, per project rule 6:

- **No Ukrainian population data.** Every prevalence figure here is US, Korean, UK, Chinese or
  African-American. The UA distribution over FFIT labels is unknown. ДСТУ ГОСТ 31396:2011 gives 356
  typical female figures on a 6 cm grid — it has never been run through these formulas. That is a
  one-afternoon job and would give a real prior instead of a borrowed one.
- **No calibration.** Nothing here is calibrated against "I would wear this" verdicts. The 3.6″, 9″,
  10″, 1.193 constants are fitted to US/Korean scan data for *pattern-making*, not to Ukrainian
  perception of whether an outfit works. They are **T1 for what they were fitted to and T3 for our
  use.** Force ≤ verification: hint, not gate.
- **Diamond/Oval landmarks still undefined.** The 2020 revision routes around the missing stomach and
  abdomen definitions rather than supplying them. If those categories ever need to be split more
  finely, the landmark problem returns.
- **Parker et al. read from abstract and repository record only.** The full text is paywalled. The
  40% figure and the shoulder finding are from the authors' own abstract; the per-shape breakdown of
  *which* categories are least stable is in the paper and would sharpen §4 considerably. Worth one
  interlibrary request.
- **Chrimes et al. §6 is n=30, one garment.** Listed as hypotheses, not rates. It has not been
  replicated and the cells are too small to support a per-shape probability.
- **Shoulders remain unhandled.** Parker et al. identify the gap; no source here fills it. FFIT will
  not give shoulder geometry no matter how carefully it is implemented.

# Ease, Silhouette and Proportion — Primary-Source Extraction (EN)

**Sections 10–14.** Same method and register as §1–9 above: every number was read out of a named
published source, and every number the sources do *not* supply is marked as still missing.

**Selection rule.** `силует.py` and `посадка.py` carry **24 constants tagged T3** — the author's
own estimates. Only sources that publish a value for one of them are extracted here. Sources that
supply no number are not extracted, no matter how often they are cited. Nothing else earned a
place.

**Convention.** All ease figures are in **inches as published**, with cm conversions shown but
never substituted. Same warning as the FFIT convention note in the header: the industry bands are
published in whole and eighth inches, and the boundaries move if you round them through cm.

---

## 10. Ease bands — the one place the sources do give numbers

### 10.1 The published table

The apparel-pattern industry has a five-band ease classification. It is printed on Vogue,
Butterick and McCall's pattern envelopes and reproduced in *Threads* #221 (Spring 2023, Louise
Cutting, "Ease Into Place", p. 71) as the **Fit and Ease** table:

| Fit description | Blouse, dress | Jacket |
|---|---|---|
| Close-fitting | 0″ – 3″ | n/a |
| Fitted | 3″ – 4″ | 3¾″ – 4¼″ |
| Semifitted | 4″ – 5″ | 4⅜″ – 5⅜″ |
| Loose-fitting | 5″ – 8″ | 5⅞″ – 10″ |
| Very loose-fitting | 8″+ | 10″+ |

The Vogue/Butterick/McCall's envelope wording differs only in where the boundaries are closed:
close-fitting 0–2⅞″, fitted 3–4″, semi-fitted 4⅛–5″, loose-fitting 5⅛–8″, very loose 8″+.

**Minimum (wearing) ease**, same source, stated separately from design ease:

- Bust **2½″ – 3″**
- Waist — dresses 1″ or more; skirts and trousers 1″ – 1½″
- Hips **2″ – 3″** (more for plus or extended hip sizes)
- Crotch depth ½″ – ¾″ (for plus or extended sizes)
- Wrist 1″

### 10.2 What this is and is not, epistemically

These are **one voice, not two.** The *Threads* table and the Big Four envelope standard come from
the same pattern-industry genealogy; the second is not independent confirmation of the first. By
the project's own source rule this is a **single-genealogy practitioner convention**: T3, not T1.
It is nonetheless a large improvement on the current state, because the current state is one
person's estimate and this is the convention an entire industry cuts to and that Ukrainian retail
size charts are downstream of.

**Conditions on it** — when it applies, how strongly, where it breaks:

- **Applies to** woven garments described by a Western pattern or RTW size chart. Knits with
  meaningful stretch are not covered; ease there is a function of fabric stretch percentage and is
  routinely negative.
- **Breaks at the top of the size range.** The source states the condition itself: minimum ease is
  for small figures, and *larger figures may need proportionally more wearing ease*. The band edges
  are not size-invariant even in the source's own terms.
- **The jacket column is not decoration.** The whole table shifts up roughly ¾″–2″ for garments
  worn over other garments. A single ease scale that ignores layer position is wrong by about one
  full band for outerwear.

### 10.3 The measured defect in the current implementation

`силует.операція()` classifies a garment by the **ratio** garment/body, with fixed thresholds
`cling < 1.00 ≤ skim ≤ 1.18 < перехід < 1.25 ≤ tent`. The published bands are in **absolute
inches**. These are not the same object, and the difference is measurable.

**Measurement 1 — what a fixed ratio actually means.** Each code threshold converted to absolute
ease across the real body range:

```
skim_стеля = 1.18     bust  80 cm ->  5.7"  loose-fitting
                      bust  96 cm ->  6.8"  loose-fitting
                      bust 120 cm ->  8.5"  VERY LOOSE

tent_межа  = 1.25     bust  80 cm ->  7.9"  loose-fitting
                      bust  88 cm ->  8.7"  VERY LOOSE
                      bust 120 cm -> 11.8"  very loose
```

`tent_межа` crosses the 8″ very-loose line at **81 cm** bust, `skim_стеля` at **113 cm** — both
inside the served range, not at its edge.

**Measurement 2 — discriminating power.** At a median bust of 92 cm, sweeping ease from −6 cm to
+30 cm (the real catalogue range from stretch knit to oversize):

```
code label   share of ease space   published categories it merges
cling            16.8%             negative ease
skim             46.0%             close-fitting, fitted, semifitted, loose-fitting
перехід          17.8%             loose-fitting, very loose
tent             19.4%             very loose
```

**`skim` swallows 46% of the ease space and collapses four published categories into one.** The
state the code describes as "the garment departs from the body just enough to give a clean line"
currently runs from a body-skimming shell all the way to a garment with 6 inches of ease. This is
the "a filter that passes most of the space is not a filter" failure, measured rather than
suspected.

**Measurement 3 — how much would change.** Reclassifying with the published cm bands instead of
the ratio thresholds, uniform prior over bust 78–124 cm and ease −6…+30 cm, N=60,000:

```
garments that change label:              48.7%
disagreement by body size:
  bust  78–90 cm    37.3%
  bust  90–106 cm   46.7%
  bust 106–124 cm   58.2%
```

The rise with body size is the signature of the ratio/absolute mismatch: a fixed ratio grants
proportionally more absolute ease to a larger body, so the larger the woman, the more generous the
code silently becomes. **Caveat:** this is a *uniform* prior over ease, not a catalogue
distribution. Real feeds cluster. 48.7% is an upper bound on how much a real catalogue would move,
and it is not a real number until the feed is loaded.

### 10.4 Falsifiable next step

Change `операція()` to take **absolute ease in cm** and cut it on the published bands, keeping the
ratio only as a fallback when the size chart gives no circumference. Then run the existing
regression suite and one real catalogue page, and check the one thing that would falsify this:
**if the new label distribution is not visibly flatter — if something still takes 45%+ of the
space — then the absolute bands did not buy discrimination and the problem is elsewhere**, most
likely in the fabric layer (§10.5).

### 10.5 What no source supplies

Every source that describes the skim window describes it through **fabric** — weight, drape,
opacity — and not through ease. *Threads* reaches the same point from the other side: the same
ease reads as a different silhouette on different cloth. The corpus already flags this, and it
remains true after this extraction: **ease bands fix the arithmetic, not the fabric dependence,
and fabric is the more likely source of false firings.** Drape is not measured anywhere in the
system.

## 11. Stripes — the missing condition

§6.1 above already carries Chen & Peng's five-spacing table with the absolute-centimetre finding,
and §7.3 already records why the audited file's bare Thompson & Mikellidou import was rejected.
What was missing from this file is the paper that supplies the **conditions**, and without it the
stripe rule cannot be scored at all.

### 11.1 Ashida, Kuraguchi & Miyoshi (2013), *i-Perception* 4(5), 347–351, DOI 10.1068/i0595rep

Three findings:

1. **The Helmholtz effect is stronger for a thin figure than for a fat one, with possible reversal
   for the fat figure.** The slimming direction is not a property of stripes; it is conditional on
   the body it is on.
2. **Large variability across participants**, suggesting the effect depends on which features an
   observer attends to.
3. **Strong hysteresis by presentation order** — judging fat figures before thin ones changes the
   result, which the authors read as an effect of the surrounding people one has just been looking
   at.

Method: n=31 undergraduates, chin rest, forced choice at ~1.8 s. Small, controlled, single-lab.

**Operational consequence.** Point 1 alone kills any unconditional stripe rule, and it kills it in
the direction that matters most commercially: **the slimming effect is weakest or absent exactly
on the bodies most likely to be asking for it.** Point 3 means the effect is not stable enough to
carry above `hint`.

### 11.2 Dating error to resolve

The block headed `KOUTSOUMPIS ET AL. — CROSS-CULTURAL STRIPE PERCEPTION, 2026` further down this
file dates the work 2026. The verified publication — Koutsoumpis, Economou & van der Burg,
*Helmholtz Versus Haute Couture: How Horizontal Stripes and Dark Clothes Make You Look Thinner*,
*Perception*, DOI 10.1177/03010066211038158 — is **2021**, and §7.3 above cites it correctly as
2021. Either the block is mis-dated or it describes a different, later paper: the
n=316 / n=419 Greece–Netherlands–Taiwan design does not match the 2021 record located. **Not
resolved; flagged rather than guessed.**

### 11.3 Net position across all four stripe sources

One usable rule, and it is a rule about **not** having a rule:

> Stripe orientation is a low-weight contextual prior at `hint`. What is measurable and worth
> storing is **absolute period in cm** (§6.1) and **garment lightness** (§6.2). Orientation on its
> own does not earn a finding.

## 12. Leg-to-body ratio — the published effect size argues against using it

`посадка.РЕЄСТР["нога_норма"] = 0.480` (T3, K-BOD-05), currently withheld because the system does
not collect crotch height.

**What the literature publishes.** LBR is leg length / total height. Versluys & Skylark (2017),
*R. Soc. Open Sci.* 4:170399, working from a validated anthropometric database: **mean LBR 0.491,
SD 0.015**. Stimuli were built at ±1, 2, 3 SD: 0.447, 0.462, 0.477, 0.491, 0.506, 0.521, 0.535.

**Optimum.** Approximately **0.3–0.5 SD above the population mean** for male figures (Versluys &
Skylark 2017; Brooks et al. 2018, *R. Soc. Open Sci.* 5:171790), consistent with Sorokowski &
Pawlowski (2008), who reported a preference for legs about **5% longer than average**.

**For female figures the literature does not agree.** Average-is-optimal: Kiire (2016), Frederick
et al. (2010). Longer-than-average-is-optimal: Sorokowski & Pawlowski (2008), Swami et al. (2006),
Bertamini & Bennett (2009), Marković et al. (2016). The direction is unsettled for exactly the
population this product serves.

**Two consequences, and the second is the useful one.**

1. The code's 0.480 sits **0.73 SD below** the published mean. As a norm, it is mis-centred by a
   large margin on the only distribution anyone has published.
2. **The effect is smaller than the measurement.** The perceptual optimum is +0.3–0.5 SD =
   **+0.005 to +0.008 in LBR**. On a 165 cm woman that is **0.7–1.2 cm of leg length**. No
   photo-derived estimate of crotch height on a phone camera resolves 1 cm. The signal is below the
   noise floor of the intended input.

Versluys & Skylark add the finishing condition: **the optimum and the sensitivity both differ
between silhouettes and realistic rendered figures.** A system judging from photographs cannot
borrow a constant fitted on silhouettes.

**Decision this changes.** `посадка.py` withholds vertical balance on the grounds that the input is
not collected. That decision was right for a weaker reason than the real one. The real reason is
stronger and should be recorded: **even with the input collected, the published effect is an order
of magnitude below what the input could measure, and its direction is not settled for women.**
K-BOD-05 stays withheld on evidence, not on missing data — and `нога_норма` should be deleted
rather than left as a target waiting for a measurement that would not help.

## 13. Shoulder tolerance — searched, not found

`посадка.РЕЄСТР["плече_допуск"] = 1.5` cm (T3, K-FIT-01): deviation of the shoulder seam from the
acromion at which the shoulder reads as wrong. The corpus calls this a purchase gate — a hard rule
— while supplying no number.

**What the search returned instead.** Apparel-industry sources publish **manufacturing tolerance**,
not perceptual tolerance: ±0.25″ (0.6 cm) for fitted garments, ±0.5″ (1.3 cm) common for casual
wear, up to ±0.75″ (1.9 cm) at looser quality tiers; shoulder width is a primary point of measure
in every spec.

**These are different quantities and must not be substituted.** Manufacturing tolerance says how
far a factory may drift from its own spec. It says nothing about how far a seam may sit from the
acromion before a person reads the garment as ill-fitting.

**The operational consequence is still real.** The code's 1.5 cm threshold sits *inside* the
manufacturing tolerance band for casual wear. A finding fired at 1.5 cm is partly firing on
production variance rather than on fit. Whatever the perceptual threshold turns out to be, it
cannot usefully be **below about 1.3 cm**, because below that the garment's own manufacturing noise
dominates.

**Register:** 1.5 cm remains T3, and K-FIT-01 must not be promoted to a hard gate on this evidence.
The corpus's own claim ("purchase gate") currently exceeds its verification.

## 17. Ease is a profile along the body, not a number

Produced by running the codebase's ease tables against the published sources of §10 and §16.
Each item below states when it applies, how strongly, and where it fails.

### 17.1 Published wearing ease is landmark-specific, and the differences are large

*Threads* #221 gives minimum wearing ease **per landmark**, not as one figure:

| Landmark | Minimum wearing ease |
|---|---|
| Bust | 2½″ – 3″ (6.4 – 7.6 cm) |
| Waist, dresses | 1″+ (2.5 cm) |
| Waist, skirts and trousers | 1″ – 1½″ (2.5 – 3.8 cm) |
| Hip | 2″ – 3″ (5.1 – 7.6 cm) |
| Crotch depth | ½″ – ¾″ (1.3 – 1.9 cm) |
| Wrist | 1″ (2.5 cm) |

**The bust minimum is roughly three times the waist minimum and three times the wrist minimum.**
This is not a rounding difference; it is the difference between a garment that can be worn and one
that cannot.

**What follows, and it is the load-bearing part.** A garment's ease cannot be represented by a
single scalar over the body — neither as a multiplier nor as an addend.

- A **uniform multiplier** grows with local girth: ×1.10 puts 9.2 cm at a 92 cm bust and 2.4 cm at
  a 24 cm ankle. The ankle figure is at or below the published wrist minimum (2.5 cm), so the same
  label that is comfortable at the bust is unwearably tight at the hem.
- A **uniform addend** fails in the opposite direction: adding the bust minimum of 7.6 cm at the
  ankle gives a 32 cm ankle opening on a 24 cm ankle.

**Correct representation.** A cut label describes an **ease profile** — a curve along the body, not
a point. A wide-leg trouser is small ease at the waist and large ease at the hem. A fitted trouser
is the reverse of a fitted top: near-minimum at the waist, more at the calf. A peplum is minimum at
the waist and maximum at the high hip. **A single number per garment cannot encode any of these.**

**When it applies:** wovens with no meaningful stretch. **Where it breaks:** knits, where ease is a
function of fabric stretch percentage and is routinely negative — the published minimums do not
apply at all. **Force:** the landmark figures are one-genealogy practitioner convention (T3, see
§10.2); the *structural* conclusion that ease varies along the body is stronger than the individual
numbers and does not depend on them.

**Before/after.** With this, "wide-leg" is a query for a garment whose ease profile rises toward the
hem, and the waist is checked against the waist minimum. Without it, "wide-leg" is one number
applied everywhere, and a trouser passes on its hem while being too tight to fasten.

### 17.2 Zero ease is not a fit category

A woven garment cut at body circumference cannot be worn: the published minimum at the bust is
2½–3″ before any design ease is added. **"Fitted" in a catalogue never means zero ease** — it means
close-fitting, 0–3″ of *design* ease **on top of** the wearing minimum.

**Where this matters:** any system that treats a "fitted" label as "garment = body" will accept
garments that cannot be fastened, and will describe genuinely close-fitting garments as loose by
comparison. **Where it breaks:** knits again, and bias-cut garments, where negative ease is normal
and intended.

### 17.3 Deriving high hip from hip silently deletes a FFIT category

§2.1 above records that the high-hip landmark is the fragile one and that the Spoon boundary rests
on it entirely. This quantifies what happens if it is *derived* rather than measured.

Sokolowski & Bettencourt's cascade separates Spoon from Bottom Hourglass on **high hip / waist ≥
1.193**. If high hip is estimated as a fixed fraction of hip — take 0.92 as an example — the
condition collapses into a hip/waist condition:

```
0.92 × hip / waist ≥ 1.193   ->   hip / waist ≥ 1.297
```

Bottom Hourglass additionally requires **hip − waist ≥ 9″ (22.9 cm)** *and* high hip/waist < 1.193,
i.e. hip/waist < 1.297. The two are then simultaneously satisfiable only when:

```
waist + 22.9 < waist × 1.297   ->   waist > 22.9 / 0.297 ≈ 77 cm
```

**Bottom Hourglass becomes algebraically impossible for any woman with a waist below ~77 cm.**
Every such body that would have been Bottom Hourglass is relabelled Spoon.

**Generalisation, which is the actual knowledge:** whenever a taxonomy separates two categories on
a landmark, deriving that landmark from another measurement does not degrade the boundary — it
**deletes** it, and does so invisibly. The resulting label distribution still looks plausible.
Any fixed fraction produces this; 0.92 is only the example.

**When it applies:** any FFIT implementation. **Where it breaks:** nowhere — this is algebra, not
an empirical claim. **Force:** hard, and the strongest statement in this section, because it does
not depend on any tier. **What to do instead:** if high hip is not measured, do not emit a label
that depends on it. Report the input as absent.

### 17.4 The broad-top / narrow-bottom body is rare, and no published sample contradicts that

A balance-pair role assignment (top girth vs hip girth, threshold ±5%) run over a synthetic
population with correlated circumferences gives:

```
narrow top / broad bottom   58.6%
neutral (below threshold)   36.0%
broad top / narrow bottom    5.4%
```

**The population is synthetic and the exact percentages are not evidence.** What is evidence is the
cross-check against the published prevalences already recorded in §2.2: across Chinese (n=400), US
(n=340), African-American (n=72) and UK (n=30) samples, **Inverted Triangle does not appear in any
of the four reported distributions.** Spoon, Bottom Hourglass, Rectangle and Triangle account for
the mass everywhere.

**Consequence for validation, not for styling.** Rules written for the broad-shouldered,
narrow-hipped body will be exercised on a few percent of users. They cannot be validated on a small
test set, they will not show up in aggregate quality metrics, and they will fail silently. They
need deliberate fixture bodies, not sampling.

**Where it breaks:** all four published samples are small or non-European, and none is Ukrainian.
§2.2's warning stands — do not seed a shape prior from them. This claim is only that no available
sample shows Inverted Triangle as common, which is weaker than saying it is rare here.

### 17.5 Method note — discriminating power must be measured on the system's own output

Recorded because it produced a false finding during this pass, and the failure is reusable.

A fit-label table was judged degenerate on the grounds that three of its five labels mapped to the
same published fit category. Measured on the system's actual output, all five labels produced
distinct garment areas, monotone and 7–14% apart. **The degeneracy was in the mapping onto
published categories — a mapping the system never performs.**

**Rule:** discriminating power is a property of the output a table actually produces, not of any
intermediate labelling applied to inspect it. Measure the thing the system emits. An inspection
category invented for the audit will happily manufacture defects that do not exist.

## 6.1 Stripe direction is not the operative variable — absolute period is

Tested on a fitted dress, white ground with black stripes, five spacings specified in **centimetres of real garment**:

```
1 × 1  :  1 cm line, 1 cm gap   equidistant   period  2 cm
1 × 2  :  1 cm line, 2 cm gap   pencil        period  3 cm
1 × 5  :  1 cm line, 5 cm gap   pencil        period  6 cm
2 × 2  :  2 cm line, 2 cm gap   equidistant   period  4 cm
5 × 5  :  5 cm line, 5 cm gap   equidistant   period 10 cm
```

Pencil = gap wider than the line. Equidistant = uniform.

Horizontal versus vertical, head-to-head, percentage choosing horizontal:

| spacing | slimmer (front) | slimmer (back) | slimmer (overall) | enhances figure | more appealing |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1×1** | 42.5–49.1 (ns) | **24.8 → vertical 75.2** | **38.3 → vertical 61.7** | **37.9 → vertical 62.1** | 56.5 (ns) |
| **1×2** | **66.8–69.2** | **65.4** | **67.3** | **60.7** | **70.1** |
| **1×5** | **65.9–66.4** | **60.3** | **62.1** | **65.0** | **73.8** |
| **2×2** | 30.8–38.8 → vertical | 48.6 (ns) | 37.9 → vertical 62.1 | 43.0 | 50.5 (ns) |
| **5×5** | **64.5–68.7** | **70.1** | **69.2** | **72** | **82.2** |

**Duty cycle does not predict the answer.** 1×1, 2×2 and 5×5 all have duty cycle 0.50 and give three different verdicts — vertical, ambiguous, horizontal. **Store absolute line width and gap width in centimetres, not a normalised ratio.**

Within horizontal stripes alone, comparing spacings against each other: **1×2 is the strongest slimming choice** (56.1% front / 61.2% back among pencil types; 36.9% / 35.0% among all five). **1×5 is the weakest** (10.7% front / 8.9% back among all five).

Within vertical stripes alone there is no consistent winner: 2×2 preferred from the front, 1×1 from the back.

**Mechanism for the failure of wide spacings:** as gaps widen the stripes become visually dispersed, the Helmholtz effect disappears, and the stripes stop functioning as a shading cue for three-dimensional body form.

*Boundary:* one model, BMI 20.8, fitted stretch dress, black-and-white only, viewed at 24–32° visual angle, forced-choice judgement, Taiwanese undergraduate observers.

# 6. PRINT AND PATTERN — the colour part

## 6.1 Print requires a full descriptor, not a motif name

Minimum vocabulary: **motif class + scale + density + direction + background + internal value/chroma contrast.** The same motif can be ditsy or statement; without scale the description is incomplete.

- **Micro-scale** below roughly 1 cm at conversational distance reads as a solid surface property, not a statement print. Counts close to a neutral in a mix.
- **Same-family mixes** (stripe + stripe, check + check) need a scale gap starting at ×2 **and** a difference in value, chroma or texture. Both, not either.
- **Internal print contrast** measured as L\* spread between dominant print colours: ≤1 value step = quiet; 2–3 = standard; >3 = loud and capable of being the focus on its own. Chroma spread assessed separately.
- **Negative space determines classification.** A garment that is 70% cool white ground with 30% small warm red flowers is primarily a **light, cool** garment. If the ground is densely filled, the print colours visually mix instead.
- **Placement:** all-over behaves as a surface property; placed motif is a full focus and spends focus budget; border print creates a termination point and inherits the rules of termination points.
- **Tone-on-tone** herringbone or jacquard behaves as texture rather than as a print and does not spend full print budget.
- **Echo of form** (curved with curved, angular with angular) is a soft coherence bonus, not a gate.
- **Pattern as palette seed:** extract two or three colours from the print and repeat them in solid pieces.

---

## 6.2 Luminance beats orientation — R-HELM-07 · default

Within the striped-garment literature the value finding is the colour finding: **lightness and luminance dominate stripe orientation.** In a controlled comparison the thinning effect of stripes **vanished entirely against a black dress** (p = .26) while holding against a light one (d = 0.61), and lower luminance produced its own independent small-to-moderate thinning effect separate from any pattern.

For this file the consequence is narrow and firm: **when a garment carries both a value decision and a stripe decision, the value decision is the larger lever.** Stripe geometry itself — orientation, absolute period in centimetres, pencil versus equidistant, the fit gate, the belief split — is body-perception, not colour, and lives in the silhouette file.

## 6.3 Print axes in full — R-PRN-01 · strong

Every print is a **motif class ⟂ axes**. A motif without a scale carries no information.

**Motif classes:** stripe · check · dot · floral · animal · geometric · paisley · abstract · conversational · logo · camouflage · **tone-on-tone textural** (behaves as texture; does not spend the print budget).

**Axes:**
- **scale in cm** — micro < 1 · small 1–3 · medium 3–8 · large > 8 (starting ranges)
- **density** — dense ↔ open
- **internal value and chroma spread**
- **direction**
- **ground** — light or dark

**R-PRN-02 · strong.** Micro scale (< ~1 cm) is **pseudo-solid**: at conversational distance it reads as texture, does not spend the print budget, and counts as a neutral in a mix.

> **Mechanism (verification run 2, 2026-09-05).** The fusion of a fine pattern into a solid is the eye's contrast sensitivity to spatial frequency (the Campbell–Robson curve): the threshold is set by the **angular size** of the motif — the pair (size, distance) — not by absolute centimetres. "<1 cm at conversational distance" remains a working proxy and stays `[folkloric constant]`, but now with its mechanism named, so it can be calibrated by experiment rather than argued about. Force `strong` unchanged.

**R-PRN-03 · soft.** Same-family mixes (stripe + stripe) require a scale gap of **≥ ×2 AND** a difference in value, chroma or texture. Both conditions, not either. Otherwise it is a frequency conflict.

**R-PRN-04 · hint.** Echo of form — curved motifs with curved, angular with angular — a soft coherence bonus.

**R-PRN-05 · soft.** Power clash, with no shared colour and no scale contract, only under fashion-forward intent. It spends risk budget and requires a solid neutral anchor.

**R-PRN-06 · soft.** Animal print in muted colouring behaves as a **print-neutral**.

**R-PRN-07 · strong.** Placement is a field, not an afterthought: `all-over / placed(zone) / border(edge)`. **Placed = a created focus** and spends focus budget. **Border = a termination point.** Without this field a placed print is wrongly scored as texture.

**R-PRN-08 · soft.** Internal print contrast is **computable**: the L\* spread between the swatch's dominant colours, expressed in value steps — **≤1 quiet · 2–3 standard · >3 loud** and capable of being the focus by itself. Chroma spread assessed separately via C\*.

**R-PRN-09 — Ukrainian gate: camouflage and military motifs.** **Split into two rules, verification run 2 (2026-09-05).** Until then it was one blurred `hint` covering everything "military", which is both too weak for the legal case and too strong for the stylistic one.

> **Source.** Ministry of Defence of Ukraine, clarification of 2026-04-18 `[✓]`: civilian "military"-styled clothing **without** a regulation camouflage pattern (ММ-14 "pixel", ММ-16Ф) and **without** insignia is permitted; imitation of uniform **with** insignia falls under art. 186-8 of the Code of Administrative Offences (fine 2 550–3 400 UAH with confiscation; on repeat 3 400–6 800 UAH or 30–40 hours of community service).

> **R-PRN-09a · `hard` · T2 (a norm of law, not of taste).** **WHEN:** the name, description or params carry a regulation pattern or insignia — "піксель", "ММ-14", "ММ-16", "мультикам", "шеврон", "погон", "нашивка ЗСУ". **FORCE:** `hard` — veto with the reason stated as *ст. 186-8 КУпАП*. **BREAKS:** genuine service uniform for a serving person is outside this system's scope. **CHANGES:** *before* — such an item could enter the pool and be offered. *after* — it is rejected with a legal reason a person can check.

> **R-PRN-09b · `hint` · T4 (appropriateness, not prohibition).** **WHEN:** "мілітарі", "хакі", "камуфляж" appear **without** the markers of 09a. **FORCE:** `hint` — a prior for the model about appropriateness in a wartime context, never a filter. **BREAKS:** khaki as a plain colour is not a military motif; the word alone is a weak signal. **CHANGES:** *before* — one blurred hint over all of "military". *after* — cargo trousers in khaki stay a soft prior, while an item with regulation camouflage is rejected outright.

## 6.4 Measured texture and shine scales — R-TEX-01, R-TEX-02 · soft

**Texture 0–3:** 0 smooth (poplin, crepe, satin) · 1 fine structure (piqué, oxford) · 2 pronounced (tweed, bouclé, corduroy) · 3 dominant (chunky knit, pile, fur). **Levels 2–3 add visual weight** and push toward the casual pole.

**Shine 0–3:** 0 matte · **1 soft lustre** — a background property that does *not* spend the budget, but there may only be one such background per outfit · 2 pronounced, spends the budget · 3 mirror or sequin, spends the budget and is almost always a focus.

Anchors are starting points, to be calibrated against a real catalogue.

## 6.5 Fabric properties that constrain a colour decision — K-MAT · C·strong

Only the parts where colour is the thing being decided:

- **K-MAT-04.** Texture mixing is **the engine of monochrome** — it is what rescues a one-hue outfit from flatness. Shine budget ≤ 1–2 surfaces (§6.4).
- **K-MAT-01.** Fabric formality must match the formality of the cut. Relevant here because the same colour reads at different formality on different cloth, so a colour judged in isolation can be right and the garment still wrong.
- **K-MAT-05.** Pilling, stretching and worn heels fail regardless of composition. **No colour decision repairs a condition failure** — colour scoring must not run on a garment that has already failed here.

# 27. FIT --- ELEMENTS AND PRINCIPLES OF FASHION DESIGN

## Fashion Institute of Technology / Museum at FIT

**Source:** The Museum at FIT, *Elements and Principles of Fashion
Design*.\
**Source type:** authoritative fashion-design education resource.\
**Research value:** high for the operational vocabulary of composition.

The previous file already contained the broad
balance/proportion/emphasis/rhythm framework. The FIT resource provides
a particularly clean, compact formulation of the five principles and
makes several relationships explicit enough to be useful as system
features.

## 27.1. Five basic design principles

FIT identifies:

1.  proportion and scale;
2.  balance;
3.  unity/harmony;
4.  rhythm;
5.  emphasis.

The four design elements are:

1.  shape and form;
2.  line;
3.  color;
4.  texture.

This gives a useful decomposition:

``` text
ELEMENTS = what the visual material is
PRINCIPLES = how the material is organized
```

For the AI Stylist, this distinction is useful because the garment
parser should first extract the elements and the composition engine
should then reason about their relationships.

## 27.2. Balance as visual weight

FIT defines balance in terms of **visual weight**.

It distinguishes:

### Symmetrical/formal balance

The two sides use corresponding/identical visual elements.

### Asymmetrical/informal balance

The two sides differ, but balance is produced by their **visual
impact**.

This is important because asymmetry does not mean absence of balance.

Operationally:

``` text
BALANCE ≠ SYMMETRY
```

Instead:

``` text
SYMMETRY = one way of achieving balance
ASYMMETRY = another way of achieving balance
```

For an outfit, this means the system should not penalize asymmetry
merely because left/right or upper/lower visual information differs.

## 27.3. Proportion and scale

FIT defines proportion as the pleasing relationship among parts and
states that design elements should be scaled to fit the overall
proportion.

For an outfit, scale should therefore be evaluated relationally:

``` text
detail scale
↔ garment scale
↔ body scale
↔ whole-outfit scale
```

A large statement element can work if the rest of the composition
provides an appropriate scale relationship.

## 27.4. Emphasis

FIT gives a particularly useful hierarchy model:

> emphasis creates the center of interest; the other elements support it
> by echoing its design message.

The focal point can be created by:

-   color accents;
-   significant shapes;
-   significant details;
-   lines converging;
-   groups of details;
-   contrast.

This strengthens the system concept:

``` text
FOCUS
+
SUPPORTING ECHO
```

rather than:

``` text
FOCUS
+
unrelated background
```

The supporting elements should participate in the same visual language
without becoming equally dominant.

## 27.5. Rhythm

FIT defines rhythm as leading the eye from one part of a design to
another through movement created by repetition of pattern or color.

This makes rhythm a **temporal/attentional property** of the static
image:

``` text
repetition
→ eye movement
→ connected reading
```

The practical implication is that repetition should be evaluated for
**direction**, not merely counted.

A repeated color appearing:

``` text
shoe → trouser detail → bag
```

can create a route.

A repeated color appearing randomly in many high-salience locations may
instead create noise.

## 27.6. Design elements and their perceptual behavior

FIT identifies:

### Color

The first element viewers respond to in a design.

Its three dimensions:

-   hue;
-   value;
-   intensity.

### Line

In clothing, line can be generated by:

-   seams;
-   openings;
-   pleats;
-   gathers;
-   tucks;
-   topstitching;
-   trims.

Straight lines can suggest crispness/tailoring.

Curved lines can imply fluidity.

### Shape / silhouette

The outline of the whole garment.

The silhouette is read from distance and should relate to body
structure.

### Texture

Surface interest created by weave and the visual/tactile appearance of
the fabric.

## 27.7. New system extraction from FIT

The composition engine should distinguish:

``` text
ELEMENT DETECTION
    ↓
RELATIONSHIP ANALYSIS
    ↓
PRINCIPLE EVALUATION
```

rather than encoding composition as a flat list of rules.

# MARILYN REVELL DELONG — THE WAY WE LOOK

## Useful source-derived concepts

### Apparel–body construct

The central conceptual contribution used by the corpus is that clothing should be evaluated as a visual construct involving the **whole dressed body**, rather than as isolated garments.

The useful unit of analysis is therefore:

`APPAREL + BODY + VISUAL CONTEXT`

rather than:

`GARMENT IN ISOLATION`.

This supports evaluating an outfit from head to toe as a single visual composition.

### Silhouette as form

Silhouette is treated as a major carrier of visual form. Garment edges, openings, divisions, and contours establish visual relationships with the wearer.

For the AI Stylist, this supports giving silhouette-level structure priority over isolated garment details when the two conflict.

### Figure-ground

DeLong's visual-analysis framework distinguishes the figure from its surrounding ground. Contrast in form, line, colour, texture, and other visual properties can make an element become a figure or recede into the ground.

Operationally, this means a styling system should decide **where attention is supposed to go** rather than assuming that every visual emphasis is a defect.

### Focus

A coherent composition generally benefits from a primary visual focus. Multiple equally strong focal points can compete and make the outfit visually scattered.

The location of the focus is itself part of the styling message.

### Coordination

Coordination is not simple matching. The components need to contribute to a coherent visual effect, relate to one another, and relate to the wearer.

This supports the corpus rule:

`COHERENCE ≠ MATCHY-MATCHY`.

### Line direction

Vertical, horizontal, diagonal, and curved lines can create different directional effects and eye movement. Their result depends on placement, construction, contrast, scale, and interaction with the other lines.

Therefore the corpus correctly treats:

`vertical = elongating`

and

`horizontal = widening`

as oversimplified if encoded as universal rules.

The exact chapter-level wording and complete internal framework of the book should not be reconstructed from secondary summaries. For a source-faithful knowledge extraction, the book itself should be obtained through a library or licensed copy.

# THOMPSON & MIKELLIDOU — HELMHOLTZ ILLUSION AND FASHION

## Main findings

For the controlled square/rectangle stimuli:

- a square made from horizontal lines was perceived as taller and narrower than an equivalent square made from vertical lines;
- a vertically striped rectangle required about **7.1% additional vertical extension** to match the perceived height of the horizontal-striped square;
- a horizontally striped rectangle needed to be about **4.5% wider** to match the perceived width of the vertical-striped square.

The researchers then tested whether the effect depended on a flat 2D representation.

They reported that the illusion persisted when the line pattern was placed on:
- a line drawing of a woman;
- 3D cylinders;
- a stereoscopically viewed half-body mannequin.

They did **not** find support for the idea that horizontal lines become “fattening” merely because they are placed on a 3D cylindrical body.

## Operational value

The corpus's rule:

`horizontal stripes = widening`

should not be hard-coded.

Stripe orientation is a **contextual perceptual variable**.

It must be evaluated with:
- garment geometry;
- fit;
- surface curvature;
- stripe width;
- stripe spacing;
- contrast;
- total silhouette.

## Evidence / limitations

The study does not establish a universal styling rule for every real garment, fabric, body, viewing distance, or outfit composition.

The exact numerical percentages belong to the experimental stimuli and should not be treated as garment-level coefficients.

## Five fit elements

The paper identifies the established five-element framework:

1. **Ease**
2. **Balance**
3. **Set**
4. **Grain**
5. **Line**

### Ease

Ease is the difference between garment dimensions and the wearer's body dimensions.

The paper distinguishes:

- **fit ease** — space required for function and movement;
- **design ease** — additional space used to create the intended silhouette.

### Grain

Grain concerns the relationship between fabric grainline and garment structural lines.

- parallel to the torso length at front and back;

### Balance

### Line

`FIT_SCORE`

# 3D CLOTHING MODELLING AND DIGITAL HUMAN RECONSTRUCTION — 2026 REVIEW

## New knowledge

The review provides a broad map of current 3D VTO limitations.

### Garment topology matters

Template-based approaches do not generalize cleanly across diverse garment topologies.

Each new topology may require:
- a different deformation function;
- expert intervention;
- new template handling.

This means garment geometry cannot be reduced to a generic “cloth surface.”

## Segmentation is a geometry problem

Garment transfer can fail because:
- hair is incorrectly included in the garment;
- body and garment are not cleanly separated;
- clothing boundaries are misread;
- texture underneath transferred garments cannot be correctly reconstructed.

## Collision and interpenetration

The review identifies:
- self-collision;
- garment-body penetration;
- incorrect collision handling;

as important failure modes.

A tight source garment transferred onto a larger target can penetrate the body if sizing is not properly adjusted.

## Current sizing limitation

The review reports that many current 3D draping systems tend toward close-fitting results.

They lack robust size adjustment for:
- oversized tops;
- baggy trousers;
- layered clothing;
- garments that intentionally stand away from the body.

## Real-time dynamic fit

Real-time 3D VTO remains difficult because realistic cloth deformation under movement is computationally expensive.

## Failure taxonomy

The review groups VTO failures into:

1. pose-induced errors;
2. texture artifacts;
3. sizing mismatches.

Sizing mismatch can create:
- body/garment collision;
- penetration;
- unrealistic deformation.

## New operational rule

A VTO validator should classify failures before scoring the result:

```text
FAILURE_CLASS
  pose
  geometry
  sizing
  collision
  segmentation
  texture
  occlusion
  dynamic_drape
```

This is preferable to one generic “bad try-on” score.

### SG-NEW-11 — Drape hierarchy

# NEW PRIORITY RULE — GARMENT INTENT MUST BE KNOWN BEFORE JUDGING FIT

A garment's observed looseness, volume or endpoint cannot be judged correctly without knowing its intended design.

Therefore:

```text
OBSERVED VOLUME
must be compared with
INTENDED VOLUME
```

not automatically with:

`BODY CONTOUR`.

This is particularly important for:
- oversized;
- cocoon;
- drop-shoulder;
- wide-leg;
- draped;
- layered garments.
